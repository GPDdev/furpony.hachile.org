Consumption, fursonas, and belonging

Does spending money mean fitting in? Some observations on commissions, fursonas, fursuits, and the social relationships that shape life in the furry fandom.

Translated from the Chinese original. Chinese original
01FURPONY

VOL. 01

The furry fandom is, in practice, a community heavily oriented toward consumption. Much of participating in it involves spending money: commissioning expensive artwork or paying for a fursuit. These are forms of consumption that are hard to avoid encountering once you get into furry culture. They share a feature: they are cultural purchases made for emotional reassurance and bring about almost no practical, tangible production.

That does not mean you stop counting as a furry if you arrive without spending money. In practice, you can use an avatar by 花仍是花, hkl, 三相, or 有兽. As long as it is not one of those overused 全兽出击 avatars—even a Changed avatar is hardly excessive—people can tell you know a little about the fandom. Nobody is going to follow you around every day demanding to know whether you really qualify as a furry fan when you have not even commissioned a character design.

(In fact, a substantial group could be called ‘fans of furry fans.’ They enjoy and appreciate furry art, character designs, and cultural goods; they attend furry gatherings and have plenty of furry friends. But they do not personally identify with a fursona.)

Nor does having little money mean you have no right to enjoy everything the fandom offers. ‘Earn it in the fandom, spend it in the fandom’ is a common pattern. At the smaller end, artists trade money back and forth through template commissions, and some develop into regular commission artists. People begin by modifying VRC avatars for others and progress to making original models. Some offer advertising services, sending a client's ads to the many groups they have joined. Others get good enough to make their own fursuits. There is even room for ‘basic science’: designing original head bases or researching new fur fabrics, earning money while contributing to the community. All of these activities can bring financial rewards while making the fandom livelier and increasing the creator's visibility.

Even so, I take a fairly traditionalist view that formally joining the furry family calls for a fursona, rather like a newborn needing household registration to gain legal recognition. But observing from the edges without a fursona is not illegal, just as people may want to try something before buying it. This is a fandom built on strong social ties. Someone might first get to know many furry friends—or only a few—feel warmth and care, and only create a fursona once their own sense of identification has reached that point. A fursona does not have to be commissioned. You can design and draw one yourself; terrible drawing skills are fine as long as others can understand it. They might even think you are actually beautiful and simply being deliberately abstract. Like most art, I think fursonas can be beautiful or ugly, accessible or difficult for a general audience to understand. But those distinctions should never be treated as caused by the professional status of the person who created the design. There may be a correlation; that is a different matter.

Consumerist thinking and ‘anti-capital’ thinking coexist in the furry fandom. By consumerism, I mean the fantasy that continually buying and owning products will deliver a good life and fulfill one's human—or furry—life goals. By anti-capital thinking, I mean the belief within the fandom that it should remain free from interference by any form of corporate capital: investors, large-scale machinery, mass-produced goods, or even a one-person company. The ideal is to preserve its supposedly pastoral, small-scale mode of production.

  • The first is a problem found in many fields: relationships between people are replaced by relationships between things. People imagine that commissioning enough artwork and acquiring all sorts of furry dolls, fursuits, clay figures, 3D works, and 2D works will make others like them. There is no such causal relationship, and the problem is not unique to the furry fandom.
  • The second holds that a community as original as the furry fandom should not be filled with industrially produced things, whether idols or fursuits—although templates and merchandise are, consciously or otherwise, excluded from that objection. This resembles the great collectors of a century or two ago, who bought rare objects to decorate their drawing rooms and display distinctive taste. Once something is no longer unique, the taste behind it must be ‘low’: ‘If the fandom stops being niche, I'm out.’

Both tendencies are linked to consuming ‘uniqueness’ and collecting large amounts of commissioned art. Yet the furry fandom is deeply social. Throwing money at it may improve your chances of being noticed, but it will not directly turn you into a fandom celebrity. Rather than pouring money in, you can still develop relationships without a fursuit or a new commission every week, or even every day. Those relationships can be public, such as a following, or private. And even if you never become a celebrity, people can still simply like you. Perhaps your fursona looks good; perhaps you are pleasant to talk to, and they think spending time with you would be fun and interesting.

A fandom celebrity does not necessarily maintain more relationships than an ordinary person. Relationships require effort from both sides, including friendships that exist only online. Even a major figure cannot wake up and personally say good morning to all 5,000 followers. Much of their apparent prominence comes from managing their social-media presence well. That depends heavily on accumulated knowledge about algorithms, social media, and public relations; it cannot simply be bought. Likewise, not becoming a celebrity does not mean you failed because you did not spend enough money.

From casual observation, fursuit owners appear to make up a very small share of the fandom, and full-suit owners a smaller share of those. We are all in the community, so why, as soon as fursuits come up, do we suddenly sound like some stray-dog financial outlet declaring that ‘the furry fandom is also known as the fursuit fandom’?? People without fursuits are the community's overwhelming base. Owning one does not grant superior ‘membership privileges.’ Fursuit technology is still far from mature, and many people hold off because suits can be inconvenient or uncomfortable to wear: they might use one only a few times after buying it and then be unable to sell it. There are many reasons. Buying a fursuit is a choice, not a credential or a required step. We should not turn an ordinary purchase into something sacred.

After all that, the point is that how much you spend is certainly not the absolute factor determining your experience in the furry fandom. A few years ago, there seemed to be disgraced fandom celebrities everywhere; lately, more ordinary people have been getting into trouble instead. What does that tell us? Nobody is a saint; everyone makes mistakes. A scandal is a problem of people, not money. Otherwise, ‘Grandpa 银碳’ would have become king of furryland long ago.
